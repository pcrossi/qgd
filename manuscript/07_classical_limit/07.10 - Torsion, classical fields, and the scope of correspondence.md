# Torsion, classical fields, and the scope of correspondence

## 1. What was effectively demonstrated

In this chapter we have demonstrated, in the physical Madelung sector:

1. the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bohm and continuity system;
2. the dimensionless parameter controlling the Bohm correction;
3. the asymptotic reduction to Hamilton–Jacobi;
4. the Hamilton and Newton equations as characteristics;
5. the transition from continuity to the Liouville equation;
6. the equivalence with the WKB hierarchy away from caustics;
7. the reduction of the cotangent potential in $S^3$ to the local $1/r$ potential;
8. the preservation of Noether conservations under explicit hypotheses.

## 2. The role of torsion in the macroscopic regime

The semiclassical limit does not force torsion to vanish. There are at least three distinct regimes:

- unpolarized ensemble, in which the average torsion can cancel;
- polarized medium or rotating body, in which an average component can survive;
- topological core, in which circulation remains relevant even when the motion of the center is classical.

Therefore, the transition from the Bismut connection to Levi-Civita requires an isotropization hypothesis or an estimate of small torsion in the studied observable:

$$
\langle H\rangle_{\rm macro}\simeq0.
$$

This equality is not universal. If the experiment is sensitive to polarization, spin, or vorticity, the torsional sector must remain in the reduction.

## 3. Classical electromagnetic fields

The vector construction relates the internal connection, the Noether current, and the macroscopic projection of the curvature tensor. This transition is not a consequence of the scalar Hamilton–Jacobi equation, but it can be used because the connection sector has already been constructed separately. The chain is

$$
\text{Official Hessian in the }U(1)_Q\text{ mode}
\longrightarrow
\text{effective connection}
\longrightarrow
F=dA
\longrightarrow
\text{dynamical equation and normalization}.
$$

The homogeneous equations follow from $F=dA$ and $dF=0$. The quadratic form of the mode and the conserved current produce the source equations. The next section details this correspondence, leaving only the absolute normalization of the coupling dependent on the global Hessian already discussed in Chapter 6.

## 4. Classical gravitation

The metric construction uses the Hessian of $f$, its hydrodynamic decomposition, and the dimensional analysis of the coupling. This structure will be incorporated in Section 7.12. The correspondence uses:

1. Lorentzian reconstruction;
2. admissible macroscopic background;
3. average or reduction of the torsional sector;
4. response of the metric Hessian;
5. identification of the conserved effective tensor;
6. independent calculation of the gravitational normalization.

These elements allow us to formulate the metric constitutive law and verify that its trace-reversed form is equivalent to Einstein's equations. The absolute evaluation of $G$ remains a global normalization problem, not an algebraic flaw of the classical limit.

## 5. What was not necessary

It was not necessary to modify the official action, make $\hbar$ physically disappear, identify $\tau$ with $it$, postulate trajectories prior to Hamilton–Jacobi, erase the statistical density, or assume that all macroscopic torsion is zero.

## 6. Verdict

The correspondence principle is **conditionally closed in the regular Madelung sector**. The controlling condition is

$$
\varepsilon_{\rm cl}
=\frac{\hbar}{pL_\rho}\ll1,
$$

accompanied by control of derivatives, the boundary, and caustics. In this regime, QGD recovers Hamilton–Jacobi, Hamilton, Newton, and Liouville with a Bohm error of order $O(\varepsilon_{\rm cl}^2)$ on the energy scale.

Maxwell and Einstein are correspondences of their own sectors and are unpacked in the following two sections using the vector and metric constructions already prepared. The absolute normalizations of the couplings and possible torsional residuals remain dependent on the corresponding backgrounds and observables.
