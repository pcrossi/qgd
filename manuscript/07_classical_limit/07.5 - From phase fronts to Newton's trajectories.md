# From phase fronts to Newton's trajectories

## 1. Characteristics of the Hamilton–Jacobi equation

Write the limit equation as

$$
\partial_tS_0+H(x,\nabla S_0,t)=0.
$$

Define, along a curve $x(t)$,

$$
p_i(t)=\partial_iS_0(x(t),t)
$$

and choose the velocity of the characteristic as

$$
\dot x^i=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}.
$$

Differentiating $p_i$ along the curve,

$$
\dot p_i
=\partial_i\partial_tS_0
+\dot x^j\partial_j\partial_iS_0.
$$

From the spatial derivative of the Hamilton–Jacobi equation,

$$
\partial_i\partial_tS_0
+\frac{\partial H}{\partial x^i}
+\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_j}
\partial_i\partial_jS_0=0.
$$

The terms involving the Hessian of $S_0$ cancel, and we obtain

$$
\boxed{
\dot x^i=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i},
\qquad
\dot p_i=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial x^i}.
}
$$

## 2. Newton's second law

For $H=|p|^2/(2m)+V(x,t)$,

$$
\dot x=\frac{p}{m},
\qquad
\dot p=-\nabla V.
$$

Since $p=m\dot x$,

$$
\boxed{
m\ddot x=-\nabla V.
}
$$

Newton's second law was not added: it is the equation of the phase characteristics when the Bohm term is negligible.

## 3. The correction before the limit

If we keep $Q_B$, the same derivation yields

$$
m\ddot x=-\nabla V-\nabla Q_B.
$$

Thus, the difference between the phase trajectory of the Madelung dynamics and the classical trajectory is the force $\mathbf F_B=-\nabla Q_B$. The correspondence test is quantitative: one must verify that this force is small compared to the classical force in the region and time interval considered.

## 4. Single trajectory and localized packet

Hamilton's equations provide a trajectory for each initial datum. For a body to be represented by a single trajectory, the initial distribution must be narrow and remain narrow during the observation. If there is large dispersion, the limit is still classical, but describes an ensemble.

$$
\text{semiclassical limit}
+\text{localization in phase space}
\Longrightarrow
\text{effective classical trajectory}.
$$
