# Two path integrals

Statistical mechanics and quantum mechanics utilize a seemingly common mathematical idea: instead of tracking only one trajectory, they consider an entire set of possible paths. However, the formal similarity conceals a decisive difference. The two formalisms do not assign the same type of weight to the paths and, therefore, do not immediately define the same type of integral.

This difference constitutes the initial problem of this manuscript.

## The Wiener integral

The Wiener integral describes diffusive processes, with Brownian motion as the fundamental example. Its central mathematical object is a positive probability measure over a space of continuous paths. For a functional $F[x]$ of the trajectory, we write schematically:

$$
\mathbb E_W[F]
=\int F[x]\,dW[x],
$$

where $dW[x]$ is the Wiener measure.

Wiener constructed this functional space from the distributions of Brownian increments and their Gaussian structure^[[[../ref/Wiener 1923 - Differential-Space|Wiener 1923, p. 133]]].

In a temporal discretization, successive increments possess real and positive Gaussian weights. For this reason, the integral admits the usual interpretation of statistical average: events possess non-negative probabilities and the total measure can be normalized.

It is common to heuristically represent its continuous weight by:

$$
\exp\left[
-\frac{1}{4\nu}
\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\dot x(t)^2dt
\right],
$$

where $\nu$ is a diffusion coefficient. This expression must be read with care: typical Brownian trajectories are continuous but not differentiable. The rigorous measure is constructed from the Gaussian distributions of its increments, not from the ordinary existence of $\dot x(t)$ along each path.

A gradual explanation of this distinction is in [[../notes/analysis/Measures and integrals in path spaces|Measures and integrals in path spaces]].

## The Feynman integral

In Feynman's formulation, the quantum propagator is formally represented by a sum over trajectories:

$$
K(x_1,t_1;x_0,t_0)
=\int_{x(t_0)=x_0}^{x(t_1)=x_1}
\exp\left(\frac{i}{\hbar}S[x]\right)\mathcal D x.
$$

The action of the path is:

$$
S[x]
=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}L(x,\dot x,t)dt.
$$

The weight

$$
\exp\left(\frac{i}{\hbar}S[x]\right)
$$

is not a probability. It is a complex phase of unit modulus. Different paths can contribute with phases that reinforce or cancel each other. Quantum interference depends precisely on this complex composition.

This distinction is explicit in Feynman's original formulation: the probability is the squared modulus of a sum of complex contributions, while each path contributes with a phase proportional to the classical action^[[[../ref/Feynman 1948 - Space-Time Approach to Non-Relativistic Quantum Mechanics|Feynman 1948, p. 367]]].

For this reason, the expression $\mathcal D x$ cannot be interpreted, without additional constructions, as a positive probability measure analogous to the Wiener measure. In different classes of systems, the Feynman integral can be defined by discretization, analytical continuation, oscillatory integrals, or spectral methods. The compact symbol hides these choices.

## Formal similarity and structural difference

The two integrals can be compared as follows:

| Aspect | Wiener | Feynman |
|---|---|---|
| Physical object | diffusion and stochastic averages | quantum amplitudes |
| Weight | real and positive | complex and oscillatory |
| Composition | sum of probabilities | sum of amplitudes |
| Interference | absent in the positive measure | consequence of the phases |
| Mathematical construction | probability measure | oscillatory integral or equivalent construction |

The difficulty does not consist simply in one of the expressions having $i$ and the other not. The point is that positivity and oscillation belong to different mathematical structures. A positive measure controls convergence and allows direct probabilistic interpretation; a complex phase preserves interference information but depends on cancellations that are not probabilities.

## The problem guiding the construction

The relation between diffusion and quantum propagation is profound. Under appropriate conditions, a continuation to Euclidean time transforms oscillatory propagators into diffusive semigroups. However, this transition requires hypotheses about analyticity, spectrum, contours, boundary conditions, and positivity of reconstruction. It cannot be treated as a mere typographical swap between $t$ and $-i\tau$.

The connection between Wiener functionals and evolution operators was developed under explicit hypotheses on the potential, such as positivity or lower boundedness^[[[../ref/Kac 1949 - On Distributions of Certain Wiener Functionals|Kac 1949, p. 1]]].

The initial problem can then be formulated:

> How to construct a dynamics that simultaneously preserves the probabilistic control associated with diffusion and the phase information necessary for quantum interference, without confusing a positive measure with a complex amplitude?

GDQ will begin by investigating whether there is a dynamic structure from which diffusive positivity and quantum phase can emerge as compatible regimes, rather than formalisms linked only by an external substitution.

In the next section, we will examine precisely the operation that usually connects these regimes: Wick rotation. The goal will not be to discard it, but to separate its real mathematical content from the excessively fast reading according to which it is sufficient to swap $t$ for $-i\tau$ to solve the problem.
