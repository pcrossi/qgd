# The effectiveness of mathematics in describing nature

There is an elementary fact at the origin of all theoretical physics: nature can be described mathematically. Movements, fields, waves, probabilities, and transformations can be represented by abstract relations that not only organize what has already been observed, but frequently anticipate yet unknown phenomena. An equation constructed from a few principles can remain valid far beyond the experiences that initially motivated it.

This correspondence is so habitual in scientific work that its singularity may pass unnoticed. Mathematics is developed through definitions, operations, and inference rules. Many of its structures are born from internal problems, from requirements of consistency or criteria of elegance, without any immediate physical application. Even so, some of these constructions reappear later as the exact language of natural processes.

In 1960, Eugene P. Wigner celebratedly formulated this problem in the essay *The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Mathematics in the Natural Sciences*, published in *Communications on Pure and Applied Mathematics*. For Wigner, the extraordinary utility of mathematics in the natural sciences constitutes something close to a mystery: there is no obvious logical necessity that forces concepts elaborated by human intelligence to coincide so precisely with the structure of the physical world^[[[../ref/Wigner 1960 - The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Mathematics|Wigner 1960, p. 2]]].

The adjective *unreasonable* does not mean that the use of mathematics is irrational or arbitrary. It indicates that its success exceeds what would be reasonable to expect from a tool created to organize concepts. Mathematics does not act only as an economical system of description. In historically important cases, the mathematical formulation has successfully extended to phenomena that did not directly participate in its initial construction.

Newtonian gravitation offers a fundamental example. A relation formulated from a limited set of terrestrial and astronomical observations acquired a much larger scope than the data used in its construction. The same structure began to describe the fall of bodies, the motion of the Moon, planetary orbits, and the trajectories of comets. Mathematics did not just summarize independent observations: it revealed that they belonged to the same phenomenon^[[[../ref/Wigner 1960 - The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Mathematics|Wigner 1960, p. 5]]].

Quantum mechanics made this situation even more profound. Mathematical objects initially distant from everyday experience — complex spaces, operators, eigenvalues, amplitudes, and non-commutativity relations — proved capable of predicting atomic spectra and transition probabilities with extraordinary precision. The formal structure seemed to know more about the behavior of matter than the classical intuition that had guided its investigation^[[[../ref/Wigner 1960 - The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Mathematics|Wigner 1960, p. 5]]].

However, the effectiveness of a formulation does not eliminate the need to understand it. A theory can predict correctly and still leave open the physical meaning of its elements. It can provide very precise operational rules without clarifying why those rules have exactly that shape, why certain structures are fundamental, or how different regimes emerge from the same physical reality.

This distinction between predicting and understanding guides this manuscript. Quantum Geometrodynamics does not start from the idea that mathematics, by itself, is nature. It starts from a more restricted hypothesis: when a mathematical structure describes very different phenomena with persistent precision, this effectiveness may indicate that it preserves real relations of the physical system, even if its original interpretation is incomplete.

From this perspective, geometry is not chosen only because it offers a convenient language. It is investigated as a possible dynamic structure of matter. Curvature, flow, phase, density, topology, and causality cease to be isolated instruments and are examined as aspects of the same construction. The goal is not to replace experimental results with formal elegance, but to discover if known regularities can be deduced from a smaller number of geometrically related principles.

The starting point of GDQ is, therefore, simultaneously physical and methodological. If mathematics has such a wide effectiveness in describing nature, a fundamental theory must seek not only equations that reproduce the data, but a structure capable of explaining why these equations connect. The question is no longer just which formalism works, but includes a more demanding question: what mathematical organization allows us to understand, in the same dynamics, the geometry of space, the existence of matter, and the laws that govern its evolution?

It is from this question that we will build the initial problem.
