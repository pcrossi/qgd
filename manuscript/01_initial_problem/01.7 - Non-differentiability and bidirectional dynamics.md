# Non-differentiability and bidirectional dynamics

There is a second difficulty in trying to directly identify the Wiener and Feynman integrals. The trajectories that support the Wiener measure do not possess, in general, the regularity presupposed by the classical mechanical action.

## Brownian trajectories

For a Wiener process, a typical increment satisfies the scaling law:

$$
\Delta x\sim\sqrt{\Delta t}.
$$

If we try to form an ordinary velocity, we obtain:

$$
\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}
\sim
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta t}},
$$

which does not have a finite limit when $\Delta t\to0$. Almost every Brownian trajectory is continuous and non-differentiable. Therefore, expressions like:

$$
\int \dot x(t)^2dt
$$

cannot be interpreted literally over a typical Wiener trajectory. The continuous Gaussian weight is a heuristic abbreviation of a construction by increments, cylinders, or limits of discretizations.

Wiener starts precisely from the irregular character of Brownian motion and the problem of the non-existence of tangents; later on, he formulates in terms of null measure the deviation from the typical non-differentiability coefficient^[[[../ref/Wiener 1923 - Differential-Space|Wiener 1923, p. 133]]]^[[[../ref/Wiener 1923 - Differential-Space|Wiener 1923, p. 167]]].

## The contrast with the classical action

The action used in Feynman's formulation is inspired by differentiable classical trajectories and by a Lagrangian that depends on $x$ and $\dot x$. The path integral, however, is not a sum only over classical solutions. Its definition must explain how the action functional acts on paths that can be very irregular.

We have, thus, two simultaneous differences:

| Structure | Wiener | Feynman |
|---|---|---|
| Weight | positive | complex oscillatory |
| Typical trajectory | continuous and non-differentiable | defined by oscillatory limit, not by a real Wiener measure |
| Composite object | probabilities | amplitudes |
| Evolution | diffusive semigroup | unitary group in the physical domain |

Therefore, it is not enough to transform the exponent. It is necessary to also control the path space and the meaning of the derivatives in it.

## Directional mean derivatives

A known way to treat Brownian irregularity is to replace the instantaneous velocity with conditional mean derivatives. A forward and a backward derivative are introduced, schematically:

$$
D_+x(t)
=
\lim_{h\to0^+}
\mathbb E\left[
\frac{x(t+h)-x(t)}{h}
\mathrel{\Big|}\mathcal P_t
\right]
$$

and

$$
D_-x(t)
=
\lim_{h\to0^+}
\mathbb E\left[
\frac{x(t)-x(t-h)}{h}
\mathrel{\Big|}\mathcal F_t
\right].
$$

Here, $\mathcal P_t$ represents the past information, used in the forward derivative, and $\mathcal F_t$ represents the future information, used in the backward derivative. These derivatives do not make each path differentiable. They define regular mean fields over the statistical ensemble.

The forward and backward mean derivatives are explicitly constructed by Nelson as conditional expectations with respect to the past and the future^[[[../ref/Nelson 1967 - Dynamical Theories of Brownian Motion|Nelson 1967, p. 66]]]^[[[../ref/Nelson 1967 - Dynamical Theories of Brownian Motion|Nelson 1967, p. 78]]].

We can then form two combinations:

$$
v=\frac{D_+x+D_-x}{2}
$$

and

$$
u=\frac{D_+x-D_-x}{2}.
$$

The first describes the mean current; the second records the diffusive asymmetry. This decomposition shows that transport and diffusion can coexist in the same ensemble without assigning a classical velocity to each trajectory.

In Nelson's notation, these combinations correspond to the current and osmotic velocities, with the latter related to the logarithmic gradient of the density^[[[../ref/Nelson 1967 - Dynamical Theories of Brownian Motion|Nelson 1967, p. 87]]].

Under the flow and boundary conditions explained in the note, we obtain:

$$
u= \nu\nabla\ln\rho.
$$

When $\nu=\hbar/(2m)$, this velocity recovers exactly the combination of derivatives of the density present in the quantum potential.

In this chapter, it must be understood as a known mathematical reduction, not as a foundation already derived from GDQ. The identification of $v$ and $u$ with geometric fields and the choice of the diffusion coefficient will still need to emerge from the official construction.

## Time orientation and causality

The two derivatives are conditioned on opposite temporal orientations. This offers the mathematical language necessary to compare retarded and advanced branches, but it does not prove, by itself, that physical influences propagate from the future to the past. A causal interpretation still requires a boundary prescription that combines the branches and selects which data can act as sources.

In GDQ, the future complex causal variable must relate these orientations without confusing probabilistic conditioning with retrocausal signaling. This is a construction requirement, not an automatic consequence of Nelson.

## What must be preserved by the theory

The difficulty of non-differentiability adds four requirements to the initial problem:

1. define the dynamics without requiring point-like velocity of the Wiener trajectories;
2. recover a regular physical current;
3. preserve the phase information responsible for quantum interference;
4. explain why the macroscopic limit admits approximately differentiable trajectories.

The problem no longer consists only in converting a real time into an imaginary one: it is necessary to relate path weights, boundaries, causality, domains, and path regularity. Before introducing geometry in flow, it remains to investigate whether the diffusion coefficient can be universal and whether the dependence on mass can emerge as a geometric response.
