# From diffusion to geometry

The comparison between Feynman and Wiener integrals reveals more than a difference between real and complex weights. It points to two distinct forms of mathematical evolution. On the one hand, there are diffusive processes, in which irregularities are progressively smoothed out. On the other hand, there are oscillatory dynamics, in which phase information must be preserved.

It was this difference that motivated the search for a structure capable of containing both behaviors without reducing them to a simple formal change of variable.

## Diffusion and semigroup

A diffusion equation can be written schematically as:

$$
\frac{\partial u}{\partial\tau}
=-Hu,
$$

where $H$ is a positive spatial elliptic operator in the appropriate domain. Its formal solution is:

$$
u(\tau)=e^{-\tau H}u(0).
$$

The operator $e^{-\tau H}$ forms a semigroup. As $\tau$ increases, modes associated with larger eigenvalues are more strongly damped. This is the mathematical structure that supports the heat kernel and the Wiener measure.

## Quantum evolution and phase preservation

In non-relativistic quantum dynamics, the same spatial operator can appear in the equation:

$$
i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}
=H\psi.
$$

The formal evolution is:

$$
\psi(t)=e^{-itH/\hbar}\psi(0).
$$

Now the eigenvalues of $H$ produce phase rotations, not damping. Under the appropriate hypotheses of self-adjointness, the norm is preserved. The Wiener sector organizes the diffusion; the Feynman sector organizes the interference.

Schrödinger's equation is usually classified as dispersive, not as hyperbolic in the strict sense of relativistic wave equations. The elliptic–hyperbolic opposition appears directly when we compare Euclidean operators with Lorentzian operators, as occurs in the Klein–Gordon equation. An introduction to these classifications is in [[../notes/analysis/Elliptic, parabolic, hyperbolic and dispersive equations|Elliptic, parabolic, hyperbolic and dispersive equations]].

## The reach and limits of Wick rotation

Formally, the substitution

$$
t=-i\tau
$$

transforms the oscillatory evolution into a damped evolution:

$$
e^{-itH/\hbar}
\longrightarrow
e^{-\tau H/\hbar}.
$$

This observation explains why Euclidean methods are so useful in quantum physics. However, the substitution does not constitute, by itself, a proof of equivalence between the two theories. It is necessary to control:

- the analyticity of the continuation;
- the spectrum of the operator;
- the initial and boundary conditions;
- the choice of domain;
- the positivity of the Euclidean formulation;
- the reconstruction of the physical evolution.

Historically, Wick obtained the transition to the imaginary axis by analytical continuation and deformation of the integration contour, under boundary conditions and control of the singularities — not by a purely formal substitution^[[[../ref/Wick 1954 - Properties of Bethe-Salpeter Wave Functions|Wick 1954, p. 1124]]]^[[[../ref/Wick 1954 - Properties of Bethe-Salpeter Wave Functions|Wick 1954, p. 1129]]].

Thus, Wick rotation identifies a deep formal relationship, but leaves open what dynamic structure guarantees this relationship.

## The choice of a geometry in flow

The decisive step was to realize that diffusion did not need to occur over a rigid space. Geometry itself could participate in the evolution. In this case, the object being smoothed would not only be a distribution defined over space, but the metric that determines distances, volumes, and curvatures.

The geometric flow associated with Ricci assumes, in its basic form:

$$
\frac{\partial g_{ij}}{\partial\tau}
=-2R_{ij}.
$$

Hamilton introduced the evolution in its volume-normalized form; Perelman explicitly writes the unnormalized form above and takes it as the starting point of his variational construction^[[[../ref/Hamilton 1982 - Three-manifolds with positive Ricci curvature|Hamilton 1982, p. 259]]]^[[[../ref/Perelman 2002 - The Entropy Formula for the Ricci Flow|Perelman 2002, p. 1]]].

This equation has a parabolic character after the appropriate treatment of the diffeomorphism freedom. Curvature regions evolve, and the geometry can approach special configurations determined by stationary equations.

The variational formalism developed by Perelman makes this relationship even more suggestive. In it appear jointly:

1. a metric evolution of parabolic character;
2. a conjugate heat equation for the weighted density;
3. monotonic functionals that control the evolution;
4. solitons and critical points described by equations of elliptic nature.

The weighted functional, its interpretation as a gradient flow, and the conjugate heat equation appear directly in Perelman's development^[[[../ref/Perelman 2002 - The Entropy Formula for the Ricci Flow|Perelman 2002, p. 5]]]^[[[../ref/Perelman 2002 - The Entropy Formula for the Ricci Flow|Perelman 2002, p. 8]]].

Therefore, geometry in flow offers an internal bridge between diffusion, monotonicity, and stationary structure:

$$
\text{parabolic evolution}
\longrightarrow
\text{variational control}
\longrightarrow
\text{elliptic critical geometry}.
$$

This bridge does not automatically produce Lorentzian or unitary dynamics. It does provide, however, a geometric mechanism in which the diffusive part ceases to be an external technique and starts to participate in the constitution of the system itself.

## Why Madelung and Nelson point to Perelman

The previous steps produced three objects that reappear naturally in a weighted geometry:

1. a positive density $\rho$;
2. a phase that generates current;
3. a logarithmic gradient $\nabla\ln\rho$ associated with diffusion.

If we write locally

$$
\rho=e^{-f_R},
$$

then

$$
\nabla\ln\rho=-\nabla f_R.
$$

Thus, the osmotic velocity can be represented by a gradient of the field that weights the measure. In Perelman's functionals, an exponential density and a conjugate heat equation evolve along with the metric. This correspondence does not prove GDQ, but it explains why the geometric flow is a structurally more adequate extension than a diffusion over a rigid background.

## Why torsion will be necessary

Perelman's formalism is initially formulated for Riemannian geometry without the Hermitian torsion subsequently adopted by GDQ. A complex structure with circulation and internal flow requires a compatible connection capable of registering an antisymmetric part. GDQ will investigate the generalization by a Hermitian connection with torsion.

In this chapter, this is only an architectural requirement:

$$
\text{Perelman without torsion}
\longrightarrow
\text{reference weighted flow},
$$

$$
\text{GDQ with torsion}
\longrightarrow
\text{generalization to be derived from the official action}.
$$

It is not assumed that every monotonicity theorem remains valid without revising signs, torsion closing conditions, boundary, and regularity.

## Geometric flow, NESS and physical time

The language of non-equilibrium steady state preserves an important intuition of the original text, provided that three levels are separated. The parameter $\tau$ orders the geometric flow; the time $t$ orders the reconstructed physical registers; and a NESS is an effective state in which macroscopic observables remain stationary although there are currents and entropy production after coarse-graining.

Thus, a configuration can satisfy:

$$
\partial_t\langle O_{\rm macro}\rangle=0
$$

and still have non-zero flow. This does not automatically make $\tau$ a physical fifth dimension, nor does it identify geometric monotonicity with the unitary evolution of the laboratory. The correct formulation is:

$$
\text{closed microscopic dynamics}
\longrightarrow
\text{projection onto collective variables}
\longrightarrow
\text{NESS and effective irreversibility}.
$$

The GDQ formalism comports this chain because it contains measure, flow, spectral projection, and stationary sectors; observable irreversibility further requires the apparatus, the elimination of unmonitored degrees, and a causal dynamics in $t$.

## What geometry resolves — and what it does not resolve yet

The initial intuition can now be formulated with precision. If a quantum theory requires both probabilistic control in the Euclidean sector and phase conservation in the physical sector, these behaviors may not need to be linked only by a formal rotation of time. They can correspond to different regimes of the same geometric structure.

The geometric flow provides three relevant elements for this hypothesis: a diffusive evolution, a variational control, and stationary configurations. It does not provide, by itself, the Feynman amplitude, the Lorentzian signature, the causal prescription, or the unitary reconstruction. These elements must be constructed and demonstrated in subsequent chapters.

This is the reason for not ending the chapter with a simple identification between diffusion and quantum mechanics. What was obtained here is more restricted and more useful: a precise list of the objects that must coexist in a geometric theory of matter. From here on, the question is no longer "which integral is correct?" but rather "which geometry admits these two readings without contradiction?".

## Final statement of the initial problem

The problem of this chapter does not consist in choosing between Wiener and Feynman. Both constructions encode indispensable and distinct physical properties. The problem consists in explaining their relationship without improperly identifying a positive measure with a complex amplitude.

A solution must show, within a single construction and not just by formal analogy:

1. how the boundary term is continued;
2. how states and observables transform;
3. which domain, boundary, and causal prescription allow passing from one sector to the other;
4. under what conditions the analytical continuation is reversible;
5. how physical unitary evolution is reconstructed from the Euclidean sector;
6. how continuity and Hamilton–Jacobi emerge from the same complex variable;
7. how microscopic non-differentiability produces regular mean fields;
8. whether universal diffusion and geometric inertia follow from the background or are just a parameterization;
9. how torsion modifies the flow without destroying positivity and stability.

These requirements are not just editorial cautions: Osterwalder and Schrader formulate conditions of covariance, positivity, symmetry, and clustering to reconstruct a physical theory from Euclidean functions^[[[../ref/Osterwalder and Schrader 1973 - Axioms for Euclidean Green Functions|Osterwalder–Schrader 1973, p. 584]]].

The geometry in flow is, at this point, a candidate to organize this structure, not the already demonstrated solution. The difficulties of boundary, Madelung, non-differentiability, and universal diffusion have been transformed into explicit requirements. The following chapter can then introduce the geometric domain without hiding which problems it effectively needs to resolve.

In other words, Chapter 1 does not postulate GDQ: it justifies why a geometrization is a natural response to the Wiener–Feynman conflict. The geometrization itself begins in the next chapter.
