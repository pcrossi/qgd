# Gauge, boundary terms and Wick rotation

The difference between the Feynman and Wiener integrals is not only in the sign of the exponent. It also appears in the way boundary data participate in each construction. This point becomes visible when we compare two Lagrangians that differ by a total derivative.

## Total derivatives in the action

Consider:

$$
L'(x,\dot x,t)
=L(x,\dot x,t)+\frac{dF(x,t)}{dt}.
$$

Between fixed endpoints, the corresponding actions satisfy:

$$
S'[x]
=S[x]+F(x_1,t_1)-F(x_0,t_0).
$$

The total derivative does not modify the Euler–Lagrange equations in the interior of the interval. It modifies, however, the value of the action at the endpoints. For this reason, its irrelevance to the local equations does not mean it can be erased from a path integral without examining the states and boundary conditions.

## The boundary term in the oscillatory sector

In the Feynman propagator, the transformation produces:

$$
K'(x_1,t_1;x_0,t_0)
=
\exp\left[
\frac{i}{\hbar}
\bigl(F(x_1,t_1)-F(x_0,t_0)\bigr)
\right]
K(x_1,t_1;x_0,t_0).
$$

For determined endpoints, the new factor is a boundary phase. The amplitudes and states must be transformed in a compatible manner. The physical equivalence does not stem from ignoring the phase, but from the joint transformation of the kernel, states, and observables.

This is also the elementary mechanism present in an electromagnetic gauge transformation. If:

$$
A_\mu\longmapsto A_\mu+\partial_\mu\Lambda,
$$

the coupling along a trajectory changes by an endpoint term. The wave function receives the corresponding phase. The invariant object is the complete construction, and not each factor considered in isolation.

## The boundary term in the Euclidean sector

After the continuation to Euclidean time, the oscillatory weight is replaced, when the continuation exists and the continued Euclidean term is real, by a damped weight. In this situation, the endpoint term appears as a real factor:

$$
K_E'
=
\exp\left[
-\frac{1}{\hbar}
\bigl(F_1-F_0\bigr)
\right]
K_E.
$$

A phase of unit modulus in the real sector does not remain, in general, a phase of unit modulus in the Euclidean expression. It can alter the apparent normalization of the kernel and its behavior at the endpoints. This does not demonstrate a universal breaking of gauge invariance by Wick rotation. It demonstrates that the continuation must include the boundary data and the transformation of the states.

The need for analytical and boundary conditions is already present in Wick's original construction^[[[../ref/Wick 1954 - Properties of Bethe-Salpeter Wave Functions|Wick 1954, p. 1124]]]. The reconstruction from the Euclidean sector also requires an appropriate positivity condition, as formalized by Osterwalder and Schrader^[[[../ref/Osterwalder and Schrader 1973 - Axioms for Euclidean Green Functions|Osterwalder–Schrader 1973, p. 584]]].

## The domain is part of the physical operator

A differential expression alone does not determine a physical problem. One must specify its domain and the conditions that select the admissible solutions. Schematically,

$$
\text{physical problem}
=
\text{local operator}
+
\text{domain}
+
\text{boundary conditions}
+
\text{causal prescription}.
$$

Under an analytical continuation, all these components must possess a compatible correspondence. A condition that selects retarded waves in the physical sector, for example, cannot be silently replaced by just any Euclidean condition.

## The correct conclusion

Wick rotation is more than a typographical swap, but less than an automatic equivalence. To establish the correspondence between Wiener and Feynman, it is necessary to show:

1. how the boundary term is continued;
2. how states and observables transform;
3. which domain makes the physical operator well-defined;
4. which condition selects the causal branch;
5. how Euclidean positivity allows reconstructing physical amplitudes.

The gauge thus reveals that the boundary is not a detail external to the dynamics. It participates in the definition of the problem being continued.

This shifts the question. It is not enough to compare path weights; it is necessary to understand which variable carries density, phase, and boundary simultaneously. Madelung's decomposition provides the first elementary form of this separation: a single complex variable is read as positive density plus current phase.
