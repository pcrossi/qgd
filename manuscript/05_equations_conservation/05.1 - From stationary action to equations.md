# From stationary action to equations

In the previous chapter, we presented the official action of QGD. Now we will begin to use it. The objective of this section is to carefully explain what it means to "vary the action" before performing the specific variations.

## 1. The functional and its data

The official action is

$$
\mathcal S_{\rm GDQ}
=\int_\gamma
\left[
\int_{\mathcal M_{\mathbb C}}
\frac{\hbar}{\Lambda_C^2}
\left[
\tau\left(
\mathcal R
+g^{\mu\bar\nu}\partial_\mu f\partial_{\bar\nu}\bar f
\right)
+\frac{f+\bar f}{2}-n
\right]
\mathcal U\sqrt{\det g}\,d^{2n}z
\right]
\frac{d\tau}{\tau}.
$$

To make the calculations readable, we define

$$
C=\frac{\hbar}{\Lambda_C^2},
$$

and

$$
\mathcal L_0
=\tau\left(
\mathcal R
+g^{\mu\bar\nu}\partial_\mu f\partial_{\bar\nu}\bar f
\right)
+\frac{f+\bar f}{2}-n.
$$

Thus,

$$
\mathcal S_{\rm GDQ}
=\int_\gamma\int_{\mathcal M_{\mathbb C}}
C\,\mathcal U\mathcal L_0\,dV_g\,\frac{d\tau}{\tau},
\qquad
dV_g=\sqrt{\det g}\,d^{2n}z.
$$

This abbreviation does not alter the action. It only separates three objects that vary in different ways: the density $\mathcal L_0$, the weighted measure $\mathcal U$, and the geometric volume $dV_g$.

## 2. What can vary

The central dynamic fields will be grouped in

$$
\Phi=(g,f,\bar f).
$$

However, writing $\delta\Phi$ is not enough. A variational problem also needs to declare what remains fixed. In this first derivation we keep fixed:

- the topology of $\mathcal M_{\mathbb C}$;
- the causal boundary $\gamma$;
- the complex dimension $n$;
- the regularity class of the fields;
- the boundary data that define the domain of the operator;
- the dimensionless interpretation of $\Lambda_C$ in the official action.

The complex structure and the Bismut torsion belong to the Hermitian data of the problem. They are only varied independently when an extended family of Hermitian structures is declared. We will not add an equation for the torsion by analogy with another theory.

## 3. Stationary configuration does not mean constant configuration

An admissible configuration $\Phi_*$ is stationary when, for every allowed variation $\delta\Phi$, we have

$$
\left.
\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}
\operatorname{Re}\mathcal S_{\rm GDQ}
[\Phi_*+\varepsilon\delta\Phi]
\right|_{\varepsilon=0}
=0.
$$

The term "stationary" means that the first functional derivative vanishes. It does not mean that $g_*$, $f_*$, or the density are spatially constant. A localized soliton is precisely a non-homogeneous stationary point of the functional.

We take the real part because the causal boundary can make the intermediate representation complex. The reality of the physical action depends on the class of admissible boundaries; it will not be assumed for an arbitrary boundary.

## 4. The first variation in three parts

Applying the product rule,

$$
\delta\left(\mathcal U\mathcal L_0dV_g\right)
=\mathcal U\,\delta\mathcal L_0\,dV_g
+\mathcal L_0\,\delta\mathcal U\,dV_g
+\mathcal U\mathcal L_0\,\delta dV_g.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\delta\mathcal S_{\rm GDQ}
=\int_\gamma\int_{\mathcal M_{\mathbb C}}
C\mathcal U
\left[
\delta\mathcal L_0
+\mathcal L_0\frac{\delta\mathcal U}{\mathcal U}
+\frac{\delta dV_g}{dV_g}\mathcal L_0
\right]dV_g\frac{d\tau}{\tau}.
$$

This simple formula avoids two frequent errors. The first would be to vary only the contents of the brackets of the action and forget that $\mathcal U$ depends on $f+\bar f$. The second would be to vary the curvature and forget that the volume also depends on the metric.

For a metric variation,

$$
\frac{\delta dV_g}{dV_g}
=\frac12g^{AB}\delta g_{AB}
=-\frac12g_{AB}\delta g^{AB}.
$$

The two forms are equivalent, but the sign changes depending on whether $\delta g_{AB}$ or $\delta g^{AB}$ is chosen as the independent variable. In each calculation we will use only one convention.

For variations of $f$ and $\bar f$ with fixed $z_\tau$,

$$
\frac{\delta\mathcal U}{\mathcal U}
=-\frac12(\delta f+\delta\bar f).
$$

This term will be decisive in the density equation.

## 5. From derivatives of variations to local equations

The variation of a gradient term contains derivatives of $\delta f$:

$$
\delta\left(
g^{\mu\bar\nu}\partial_\mu f\partial_{\bar\nu}\bar f
\right)
=\delta g^{\mu\bar\nu}
\partial_\mu f\partial_{\bar\nu}\bar f
+g^{\mu\bar\nu}
\partial_\mu\delta f\partial_{\bar\nu}\bar f
+g^{\mu\bar\nu}
\partial_\mu f\partial_{\bar\nu}\delta\bar f.
$$

The Euler–Lagrange equations must multiply $\delta f$, not $\partial\delta f$. Therefore we integrate by parts. Schematically,

$$
\int_M A^a\nabla_a\delta f\,dV_g
=-\int_M(\nabla_aA^a)\delta f\,dV_g
+\int_{\partial M}n_aA^a\delta f\,d\Sigma.
$$

The first term contributes to the bulk equation. The second is not algebraic waste: it determines the normal momentum and the natural boundary conditions.

After performing this procedure for all fields, the first variation takes the form

$$
\delta\mathcal S_{\rm GDQ}
=\int_\gamma\int_{\mathcal M_{\mathbb C}}
\mathcal E_A(\Phi)\,\delta\Phi^A
+\int_\gamma\int_{\partial\mathcal M_{\mathbb C}}
\Theta(\Phi,\delta\Phi).
$$

If the variations are arbitrary in the interior, stationarity implies

$$
\mathcal E_A(\Phi_*)=0.
$$

At the boundary, it is necessary to fix the fields, vanish the normal momenta, or include an interface whose variational term compensates for $\Theta$. Discarding the boundary without declaring one of these alternatives would change the problem.

## 6. What the first variation will produce

From the same action we will obtain three types of information:

1. bulk equations for density, phase, and geometry;
2. identities and currents associated with symmetries;
3. natural boundary and gluing conditions.

These results are related, but they are not interchangeable. A Noether law does not replace a stability equation, and a boundary condition cannot be chosen as if it were a bulk equation.

In the next sections we will switch to the real variables $\rho$ and $S_R$. This will allow us to separately see the deformation of the amplitude and the circulation of the phase.
