# Density variation and dynamic equilibrium

The phase variation was simple because $S_R$ does not appear in the measure. With the density, the opposite occurs: $\rho$ appears in the measure, in its own gradient, and in the explicit $-\ln\rho$ term. In this section, we will carry out the calculation without omitting any of these dependencies.

## 1. Preparation of the variation

We temporarily fix $g$, $S_R$, $z_\tau$, and the boundary. We introduce

$$
q=\ln\rho.
$$

Then

$$
\rho=e^q,
\qquad
\delta q=\frac{\delta\rho}{\rho},
\qquad
\delta\mathcal U=\mathcal U\,\delta q.
$$

We also define

$$
K_S=\frac{1}{\hbar^2}|\nabla S_R|_g^2.
$$

The real internal density of the action becomes

$$
\mathcal L_{\rho,S}
=\tau\left(
\mathcal R+|\nabla q|_g^2+K_S
\right)-q-n.
$$

The term that must be varied is, therefore,

$$
\mathcal S_q
=\int_\gamma\int_M
C\mathcal U\mathcal L_{\rho,S}\,dV_g\frac{d\tau}{\tau}.
$$

## 2. Variation of the integrand and the measure

Applying the product rule,

$$
delta\left(\mathcal U\mathcal L_{\rho,S}\right)
=\delta\mathcal U\,\mathcal L_{\rho,S}
+\mathcal U\,\delta\mathcal L_{\rho,S}.
$$

The first term is

$$
\delta\mathcal U\,\mathcal L_{\rho,S}
=\mathcal U\mathcal L_{\rho,S}\,\delta q.
$$

Since $g$, $S_R$, and $\mathcal R$ are fixed in this variation,

$$
\delta\mathcal L_{\rho,S}
=2\tau\nabla^aq\nabla_a\delta q-\delta q.
$$

Summing them up,

$$
delta\left(\mathcal U\mathcal L_{\rho,S}\right)
=\mathcal U(\mathcal L_{\rho,S}-1)\delta q
+2\tau\mathcal U\nabla^aq\nabla_a\delta q.
$$

The number $-1$ in this expression comes from the variation of the explicit $-q$ term. It would disappear incorrectly if the dependency on $f+\bar f$ were forgotten.

## 3. Weighted integration by parts

For the term with the derivative of the variation, we use

$$
\nabla_a\left(
\mathcal U\nabla^aq\,\delta q
\right)
=\delta q\,\nabla_a(\mathcal U\nabla^aq)
+\mathcal U\nabla^aq\nabla_a\delta q.
$$

Consequently,

$$
\begin{aligned}
2\tau\int_M
\mathcal U\nabla^aq\nabla_a\delta q\,dV_g
={}&-2\tau\int_M
\delta q\,\nabla_a(\mathcal U\nabla^aq)\,dV_g
\\
&+2\tau\int_{\partial M}
\mathcal U\,n^a\nabla_aq\,\delta q\,d\Sigma.
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $\mathcal U$ is proportional to $e^q$ when $z_\tau$ is fixed,

$$
\nabla_a\mathcal U=\mathcal U\nabla_aq.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\frac{1}{\mathcal U}
\nabla_a(\mathcal U\nabla^aq)
=\Delta_gq+|\nabla q|_g^2.
$$

## 4. Density equation and normalization constraint

For compactly supported variations, or after imposing boundary data that vanish the concomitant, the first unrestricted variation is

$$
\delta\mathcal S_q
=\int_\gamma\int_M
C\mathcal U
\left[
\mathcal L_{\rho,S}-1
-2\tau\left(
\Delta_gq+|\nabla q|_g^2
\right)
\right]
\delta q\,dV_g\frac{d\tau}{\tau}.
$$

If $\mathcal U$ were an unnormalized density, the arbitrariness of $\delta q$ would yield

$$
\mathcal L_{\rho,S}-1
-2\tau\left(
\Delta_gq+|\nabla q|_g^2
\right)=0.
$$

Substituting $\mathcal L_{\rho,S}$,

$$
\tau\left[
\mathcal R+K_S
-2\Delta_gq-|\nabla q|_g^2
\right]-q-n-1=0.
$$

In QGD, however, the measure satisfies

$$
\int_M\mathcal U\,dV_g=1
$$

on each admissible section. There are two equivalent ways to respect this constraint: restricting the variations by

$$
\int_M\mathcal U\,\delta q\,dV_g=0,
$$

or introducing a multiplier $\lambda(\tau)$. Using the second form, the correct equation in the normalized space is

$$
\mathcal L_{\rho,S}-1
-2\tau\left(
\Delta_gq+|\nabla q|_g^2
\right)=\lambda(\tau).
$$

The multiplier is constant over $M$ on a flow section, but it can vary along $\tau$. Its value is determined by integrating the equation along with the normalization. Thus,

$$
\tau\left[
\mathcal R+K_S
-2\Delta_gq-|\nabla q|_g^2
\right]-q-n-1
=\lambda(\tau).
$$

This is the density equation obtained directly from the real part of the official action in the normalized problem. The equation with a zero right-hand side corresponds to the unrestricted problem or to a convention where the constant has been absorbed.

## 5. How the ratio $\Delta\sqrt\rho/\sqrt\rho$ appears

Since $\sqrt\rho=e^{q/2}$,

$$
\nabla_a\sqrt\rho
=\frac12\sqrt\rho\,\nabla_aq.
$$

Applying a derivative again,

$$
\Delta_g\sqrt\rho
=\sqrt\rho\left(
\frac12\Delta_gq
+\frac14|\nabla q|_g^2
\right).
$$

Therefore,

$$
\frac{\Delta_g\sqrt\rho}{\sqrt\rho}
=\frac12\Delta_gq
+\frac14|\nabla q|_g^2,
$$

and

$$
-2\Delta_gq-|\nabla q|_g^2
=-4\frac{\Delta_g\sqrt\rho}{\sqrt\rho}.
$$

The exact equation can then be rewritten as

$$
\boxed{
\tau\left[
\mathcal R
+\frac{1}{\hbar^2}|\nabla S_R|_g^2
-4\frac{\Delta_g\sqrt\rho}{\sqrt\rho}
\right]
\text{ } - \ln\rho-n-1
=\lambda(\tau).
}
$$

The characteristic Bohm differential operator appeared directly from the variation of the weighted amplitude. The physical coefficient $-\hbar^2/(2m)$, however, only arises after the physical chart and its kinetic normalization identify $m$. We should not introduce it into this bulk equation before the reduction.

## 6. Independent verification by Fisher energy

In the non-relativistic reduction, the amplitude energy takes the form

$$
F[\rho]
=\frac{\hbar^2}{8m}
\int_\Sigma\frac{|\nabla\rho|^2}{\rho}\,d^dx.
$$

We vary $\rho\mapsto\rho+\varepsilon\eta$. Since

$$
\delta\left(
\frac{|\nabla\rho|^2}{\rho}
\right)
=\frac{2\nabla\rho\cdot\nabla\eta}{\rho}
-\frac{|\nabla\rho|^2}{\rho^2}\eta,
$$

an integration by parts yields

$$
\delta F
=\frac{\hbar^2}{8m}
\int_\Sigma
\left[
-2\nabla\cdot\left(
\frac{\nabla\rho}{\rho}
\right)
-\frac{|\nabla\rho|^2}{\rho^2}
\right]
\eta\,d^dx,
$$

when the boundary term vanishes. Using

$$
\nabla\cdot\left(
\frac{\nabla\rho}{\rho}
\right)
=\frac{Delta\rho}{\rho}
-\frac{|\nabla\rho|^2}{\rho^2},
$$

we obtain

$$
\frac{\delta F}{\delta\rho}
=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}
\frac{\Delta\sqrt\rho}{\sqrt\rho}.
$$

An integration by parts verifies that the Bohm potential

$$
Q_B
=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}
\frac{\Delta\sqrt\rho}{\sqrt\rho}.
$$

This second calculation does not replace the bulk derivation. It verifies that when the physical reduction transforms the amplitude term into the Fisher energy with the indicated normalization, the same operator becomes exactly the Bohm potential.

## 7. Hamilton–Jacobi–Bohm as a reduced equation

If the physical reduction produces the Madelung action

$$
I_{\rm Mad}
=\int dt\int_\Sigma
\left[
\rho\left(
\partial_tS_R
+\frac{|\nabla S_R|^2}{2m}+V
\right)
+\frac{\hbar^2}{8m}
\frac{|\nabla\rho|^2}{\rho}
\right]d^dx,
$$

the variation of $\rho$ results in

$$
\partial_tS_R
+\frac{|\nabla S_R|^2}{2m}
+V
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}
\frac{\Delta\sqrt\rho}{\sqrt\rho}
=0.
$$

This is the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bohm equation. Its amplitude operator was identified above in the official action; its complete temporal form remains conditional on the canonical reconstruction that will be treated in the global–local bridge.

## 8. Density boundary condition

The integration by parts left the term

$$
\delta\mathcal S_q\big|_{\partial M}
=\int_\gamma\int_{\partial M}
2C\tau\mathcal U
n^a\nabla_aq\,\delta q\,d\Sigma\frac{d\tau}{\tau}.
$$

It can be vanished by fixing $q$ at the boundary, imposing zero normal amplitude flux, or coupling the field to an interface action. Each choice defines a different spectral problem. Therefore, even the Bohm potential must be read along with its domain and its boundary conditions.
