# 23.6. Casimir as a Hessian determinant

In the ideal limit of two infinite plates separated by $a$:

$$
\Omega_a
=
\mathbb R^2_\parallel\times[0,a].
$$

The effective electromagnetic Hessian reduces to the transverse operator:

$$
K_{\rm EM}^{\rm eff}
\sim
-\partial_t^2
+
c^2(-\Delta_\parallel-\partial_z^2).
$$

The physical frequencies are:

$$
\omega_{n,\mathbf k_\parallel}
=
c\sqrt{
k_\parallel^2+\left(\frac{n\pi}{a}\right)^2
}.
$$

The universal energy per area is:

$$
\frac{\Delta E(a)}{A}
=
-
\frac{\pi^2\hbar c}{720a^3}.
$$

The coefficient $720$ is not chosen. It appears when the physical determinant is written as a regularized sum of the eigenvalues of the transverse Hessian. Formally:

$$
\frac{E}{A}
=
\frac{\hbar c}{2}
\cdot 2
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}
\int
\frac{d^2k_\parallel}{(2\pi)^2}
\sqrt{
k_\parallel^2+
\left(\frac{n\pi}{a}\right)^2
}.
$$

The factor of $2$ counts the two transverse polarizations in the ideal limit. The dimensional continuation of the integral yields the finite part:

$$
\int
\frac{d^2k}{(2\pi)^2}
\sqrt{k^2+m^2}
\longmapsto
-
\frac{m^3}{6\pi}.
$$

With:

$$
m_n=\frac{n\pi}{a},
$$

we obtain:

$$
\frac{\Delta E}{A}
=
-
\frac{\hbar c}{6\pi}
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}
\left(
\frac{n\pi}{a}
\right)^3.
$$

The sum is understood as a regularized spectral value:

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^3
\longmapsto
\zeta(-3)
=
\frac{1}{120}.
$$

Therefore:

$$
\frac{\Delta E}{A}
=
-
\frac{\hbar c}{6\pi}
\frac{\pi^3}{a^3}
\frac{1}{120}
=
-
\frac{\pi^2\hbar c}{720a^3}.
$$

The pressure is:

$$
P(a)
=
-
\frac{\partial}{\partial a}
\left(
\frac{\Delta E(a)}{A}
\right)
=
-
\frac{\pi^2\hbar c}{240a^4}.
$$

In GDQ, this is the determinant of the projected physical Hessian with an ideal boundary. The universal coefficient must not be altered by any new rule.

For real plates:

$$
\mathsf R_{\rm plate}
=
K_{YY}-K_{YI}K_{II}^{-1}K_{IY}.
$$

The physical determinant then depends on:

$$
\mathsf R_{\rm plate}(\omega,k_\parallel,T,\text{material}).
$$

Temperature enters via Matsubara frequencies:

$$
\omega_m
=
\frac{2\pi m k_BT}{\hbar}.
$$
