# 23.1. Why simple applications matter

Problems such as the well, harmonic oscillator, barrier, Casimir effect, and molecular rotor are correspondence tests. They show whether GDQ correctly reduces to the expected effective operators when the physical domain is simple.

They should not be read as complete metrological validation. In all of these cases, if we already assume:

1. flat or nearly flat background;
2. ideal domain;
3. ideal boundary;
4. reduced physical Hessian;
5. projection of physical modes;

then the known spectra must appear. If they did not, there would be an inconsistency.

The new physical content of GDQ lies in another layer: the origin of boundaries and impedances.

For any material application, the correct chain is:

$$
J_{\rm app}^{\rm class}
\to
\delta\Phi_{\rm app}
\to
\operatorname{Hess}\mathcal S_{\rm GDQ}
\to
\mathsf R_{\rm app}
\to
\text{spectrum}
\to
\text{record}.
$$

Therefore, when a problem uses a perfect wall, an ideal plate, or a rigid bond, this is a boundary idealization. GDQ accepts the idealization as a limit, but the actual material prediction requires the interface operator.
